## 3. People and roles

- **Theodore "Ted" Kaczynski (1942–2023).** Former mathematician who lived in a cabin near Lincoln, Montana. He was the UNABOM bomber.
- **David Kaczynski.** Ted's younger brother, a social worker in Schenectady, NY. He provided the tip.
- **Linda Patrik.** David's wife, a philosophy professor (Union College). Multiple sources say she was the first to suspect Ted and to press David to read the manifesto.
- **Wanda Kaczynski.** Their mother. Old family letters were kept at her home, and she joined David in providing letters.
- **Susan Swanson.** Chicago private investigator who was a friend of Linda. She took the letters to an outside analyst.
- **Clint Van Zandt.** Former FBI behavioural analyst. His team judged that the same person had probably written both the letters and the manifesto.
- **Anthony P. (Tony) Bisceglie.** Washington lawyer and family friend who contacted the FBI on the family's behalf.
- **James R. "Jim" Fitzgerald.** FBI profiler on the UNABOM Task Force from July 1995. He did the comparative language analysis. He was not a trained forensic linguist at the time, and later became one.
- **Max Noel.** FBI agent on the case, present at the arrest.
- **Jerry Burns.** U.S. Forest Service law-enforcement officer who drew Ted out of the cabin.
- **Janet Reno (AG) and Louis Freeh (FBI Director).** Recommended that the newspapers publish the manifesto.
- **Donald Graham (WaPo) and Arthur Sulzberger Jr. (NYT).** Publishers who made the joint decision. This is common knowledge but was not separately verified in this session.
- **Judge Garland E. Burrell Jr.** U.S. District Judge in Sacramento.
- **Victims killed.** Hugh Scrutton (1985), Thomas Mosser (1994) and Gilbert P. Murray (1995).

## 4. Established facts (verified in 2 or more sources)

1. **Scope.** 16 bombs between 1978 and 1995, 3 killed. The FBI and AP widely say 23 injured, but see section 9 for the conflict.
2. **Codename.** "UNABOM" was the FBI case name, from UNiversity and Airline BOMbing targets. The FBI's own wording is "university and airline bombing targets".
3. **First and last bomb.** The first was in May 1978 and was linked to Northwestern University. The last was on 24 Apr 1995 in Sacramento.
4. **The manifesto.** "Industrial Society and Its Future" was about 35,000 words long. The bomber promised to stop if one of the papers published it. The FBI and DOJ backed publication, with the real goal that someone would recognise the author (FBI 2021). It was published in The Washington Post on 19 Sep 1995 as an 8-page insert, with the NYT sharing the cost.
5. **The tip line.** Over 50,000 people called the UNABOM tip line between publication and February 1996 (FBI history page). **[single source, but official]**
6. **The family's role.** Linda Patrik suspected first. David compared the text with Ted's old writings. The family used a PI and an ex-FBI analyst, then lawyer Bisceglie contacted the FBI.
7. **The FBI's comparison.** Fitzgerald and colleagues compared the manifesto and the bomber's letters with Ted's known writings. Shared markers included British-style spellings ("analyse"; also "wilfully" and "licence" are cited), recurring ideas, and phrasing.
8. **The arrest.** 3 Apr 1996 at his cabin near Lincoln, Montana, after Forest Service officer Jerry Burns lured him outside with a ruse.
9. **What was in the cabin.**
   - Bomb components and a fully built, live bomb, wrapped and ready to mail. FBI sources say it was under his bed and had no address.
   - Tens of thousands of handwritten pages, reported as ~40,000 pages of journals.
   - Manual typewriters.
   - A typed copy of the manifesto. Contemporaneous reports differed on whether it was the original; see section 9.
10. **Cabin size.** About 10 x 12 ft (NBC Montana, Seattle Times, Deseret News and others). AETV says 10 x 14 ft, which is an outlier. The cabin is now reconstructed at FBI HQ.
11. **The plea.** 22 Jan 1998 in Sacramento federal court. He pleaded guilty in exchange for life without possibility of release, which avoided the death penalty. He waived his right to appeal. He later tried to withdraw the plea, and both the court and the appeals court refused.
12. **The sentence.** 4 May 1998: four life sentences plus 30 years, from Judge Garland Burrell Jr.
13. **His death.** 10 Jun 2023 at the Federal Medical Center, Butner, NC, aged 81. The BOP said he was found unresponsive at about 12:25 a.m. and was pronounced dead at a hospital. The BOP gave no cause of death. AP, citing four anonymous people familiar with the matter, reported suicide. **Recommended script line:** "died in federal custody in 2023."
14. **The death penalty fight.** DOJ under AG Reno sought the death penalty (announced around 15 May 1997) despite the family's campaign against it. The family saw this as a betrayal. A plea deal was reached after the court-appointed psychiatrist diagnosed paranoid schizophrenia (Cornell LII; History.com).
15. **The reward.** David received the $1 million reward in August 1998. He pledged most of it to the victims' families and needed some of it for the family's legal bills. The Unabomb Survivors Fund distributed about $630k–$680k after taxes and legal fees.

## 5. Allegations, claims and disputes

- **"Promise" about the death penalty.** Bisceglie said investigators and prosecutors led David to believe, before the arrest, that Ted's mental illness would be weighed before any death-penalty decision. This is the defence and family lawyer's account (UPI Dec 1997; Wikipedia). I found no government confirmation of any formal "assurance". **Present it as:** "David hoped to spare his brother's life; prosecutors still sought the death penalty," not as "the FBI promised."
- **David's anonymity.** Maclean's (1996) says the lawyer first gave the FBI writing samples without naming either Kaczynski. Newsweek says the family had a written FBI agreement to protect David's identity, and that the leak of his name caused friction. **[Each detail is single-source]**
- **Linda's "intuition while meditating in Paris" anecdote.** This comes from a single local-press interview (Daily Gazette). Do not use it.
- **How decisive the linguistics were.** Fitzgerald and NPR (2017) call his language work "pivotal". Inside Higher Ed notes that linguist Roger Shuy did an earlier analysis of the prose. The defence engaged Don Foster to attack Fitzgerald's text analysis. *Manhunt: Unabomber* (2017) dramatised Fitzgerald as a lone outsider. Fitzgerald himself says his screen character is a composite, and that colleagues were in reality supportive.

## 6. Evidence chain (what actually identified him)

1. The bomber's letters (especially 1993–95) and the manifesto gave the FBI a body of text, but there were no suspects.
2. Publication made the text public. The family recognised the ideas and phrasing.
   - David reportedly recalled the phrase "cool-headed logician", which Ted had once used to chide him (Maclean's/Canadian Encyclopedia 1996; Wiehl's book via Oxygen). The phrase appears in the manifesto.
3. The lawyer handed the FBI comparison material, including a **23-page essay Ted wrote in 1971** (FBI 2021 story). The first agent who read it "immediately spotted similarities". **[1971 essay: single source, but official FBI.gov]**
4. Fitzgerald's team compared the known writings with the manifesto, including the inverted idiom "you can't **eat** your cake and have it too" in manifesto paragraph 185.
5. This comparative analysis went into the search-warrant application. Secondary sources say a Montana judge accepted linguistic evidence as part of probable cause, which is often described as a first. That claim is secondary only; I did not see the affidavit.
6. The cabin search produced the physical proof: a live bomb, components, journals (which reportedly include confessions to the bombings), typewriters and a manifesto copy.

### The "eat your cake" phrase: status = REAL but DETAILS DISPUTED

- **Confirmed (3+ sources):** the manifesto uses "you can't eat your cake and have it too" (para. 185). Fitzgerald flagged it as matching Ted's known writing, and calls it a "linguistic smoking gun" (jamesrfitzgerald.com; Northeast Times 2017; JSTOR Daily; Crime Library).
- **Disputed:** where the matching usage came from. Accounts name:
  - a 1970s letter Ted signed to *Saturday Review*;
  - a 1972 letter to the *Saturday Evening Post*;
  - letters to his mother. The Crime Library says Wanda taught the boys that form of the saying.
- **Overstated:** claims that the phrase alone justified the warrant. One Salem News piece notes prosecutors said style alone was not sufficient.
- **Script-safe wording:** "Investigators noticed both texts used the same unusual twist on an old saying: 'You can't eat your cake and have it too.'" Do not name the specific letter.

## 7. Legal outcome

- **Charges and venue.** Federal charges were brought in the Eastern District of California, Sacramento. The guilty plea on 22 Jan 1998 covered the federal charges, reported as 13 counts in Sacramento and New Jersey cases. **[13-count figure: single source (Crime Magazine); verify]**
- **Sentence.** Four consecutive life terms plus 30 years, without possibility of release.
- **Other proceedings.** A later bid to withdraw the plea was denied, and the denial was upheld on appeal.
- **Death.** 10 Jun 2023, FMC Butner (died in federal custody). He had moved from ADX Florence to Butner in December 2021.

## 8. Unknown or unverified details

- Exactly which Kaczynski document contained the "eat your cake" match.

## 10. Strongest storytelling angle

**"Caught by his own words, and his own brother."**

For 17 years, the most expensive manhunt in FBI history found no fingerprints and no suspect, because the bomber built his devices from scrap. In 1995 he demanded that his 35,000-word essay be printed. The government took the gamble and let the Washington Post publish it, hoping someone would recognise the voice. One reader did: his sister-in-law, Linda Patrik. Then his brother David matched the ideas and phrases to Ted's old letters. The FBI found the same voice, down to a backwards idiom. The demand meant to spread his ideas became the evidence that ended his campaign.

## 11. Most compelling verified revelation

The manifesto was published by the government's choice, **not to make him stop but to make someone recognise him**. That is the FBI's own framing (FBI 2021 story). It worked through the bomber's own family.

The supporting detail is that his writing contained the same unusual reversal of an old saying ("you can't eat your cake and have it too") as his known writing. Mark that detail as "reported", because its exact source document is disputed.

- It is about investigation and identification, not violence, so it can be animated without gore. Use symbolic visuals: newspaper insert, highlighted words, typewriter, cabin exterior.
- The key beats are documented by the FBI itself, plus AP, NPR, Washington Post and court-history sources.
- There is a strong emotional and ethical core: David's choice and the death-penalty dilemma.
- There is high current audience interest. Netflix's film *Unabomber* (released 25 Sep 2026; Russell Crowe, Jacob Tremblay; dir. Janus Metz) debuted #1 with 16.4M views (Netflix Top 10, week of 21–27 Sep). TheWrap reports 24.9M views in week two. Earlier titles: *Manhunt: Unabomber* (Discovery, premiered 1 Aug 2017) and the Netflix docuseries *Unabomber: In His Own Words* (4 parts, 22 Feb 2020).
