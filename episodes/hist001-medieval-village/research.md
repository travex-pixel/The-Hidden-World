## 1. The penny was essentially the only coin; round halfpennies and farthings came in 1279
**Verdict: Established, with a nuance.**
- The silver penny was the only denomination struck in quantity in 1260. Edward I's recoinage began in 1279: new pennies came out in August 1279, round halfpennies only from August 1280, and farthings around 1279–80. One source records ~82.5m pennies and only ~2.4m halfpennies struck at London by the end of 1281.
- Earlier rare round halfpennies did exist. The British Museum holds a Henry III Short Cross halfpenny (reg. 2000,0907.1; moneyer Ilger, London, dated 1220–1222, Short Cross class 7a3, 0.66 g, findspot Northmoor near Standlake). Numismatic literature calls this a rare round halfpenny issue of c.1222. The BM search snippet did not itself state "round", but the 0.66 g weight fits a whole halfpenny, not a cut one. Rare round halfpennies of Henry I are also known. Rod Blunt (ukdfd) also mentions a pre-1279 farthing/early halfpenny issue.
- **Suggested wording:** "In 1260 the silver penny was essentially the only coin in everyday use. Proper round halfpennies and farthings arrived only with Edward I's recoinage of 1279–80. A few earlier experiments, such as a rare round halfpenny of Henry III around 1222, never caught on."
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - Rod Blunt, "Early Edwardian Halfpennies (1279-1344)", ukdfd.co.uk – https://ukdfd.co.uk/pages/edwardian-halfpennies/edwardian-halfpennies-p1.htm. Says no halfpennies were struck in the first period of the recoinage; the first issue was August 1280.
  - Rod Blunt, "Early Edwardian Farthings (1279-1344)", ukdfd.co.uk – https://www.ukdfd.co.uk/pages/edwardian-farthings/edwardian-farthings-p1.htm. Says farthings were introduced in the 1279 recoinage and ended the cutting of coins.
  - British Museum collection object C_2000-0907-1 – https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_2000-0907-1. A Henry III Short Cross halfpenny, London, 1220–22, moneyer Ilger, 0.66 g.

## 2. Cut halfpennies and cut farthings as small change before 1279
**Verdict: Established.**
- Pennies were cut into halves and quarters from Anglo-Saxon times until the round fractions of 1279. These were circulating, accepted small change. Several numismatic sources say most cutting was probably done officially at the mints, though cutting during transactions is also likely. "Legal tender" is a modern concept, so say "officially accepted" rather than "legal tender".
- **Suggested wording:** "To make change, pennies were cut into halves and quarters – cut halfpennies and cut farthings. Many were probably cut at the mint itself."
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - Coincraft, "Medieval Long Cross Cut Farthing" – https://coincraft.com/medieval-long-cross-cut-farthing (dealer). With Coincraft "Henry III 1216-1272" (https://coincraft.com/henry-iii-1216-1272), it says cutting was generally done at the mint and may also have happened in transactions. Cut farthings are known from c.750s until Edward I's 1279 coinage.
  - Wikipedia, "History of the halfpenny" – https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_halfpenny (tertiary). Says cutting followed the cross, and Henry I and Henry III halfpennies are rare, possibly trials.
- Background (not verified online): Royal Mint Museum and Fitzwilliam Museum (Corpus/EMC) pages discuss cut fractions. M. Allen, *Mints and Money in Medieval England* (CUP, 2012).

## 3. Voided Long Cross penny (1247): cross to the edge against clipping. Was it also a cutting guide?
**Verdict: Established for anti-clipping. "Cutting guide" is a reasonable reconstruction, not an official stated purpose.**
- The Royal Mint (shop page) says the design was introduced in 1247 to replace worn Short Cross pennies, and the long cross made clipping more obvious if its ends were cut off. Rod Blunt likewise frames it as anti-clipping and calls Long Cross "the last type officially cut into halves and quarters".
- The idea that the cross was meant as a guide for cutting appears in dealer and popular sources (e.g. Coincraft: "guidelines for cutting the coin in half…or in quarters"). Cut Long Cross fractions are in fact almost always cut along the cross arms, so showing a coin cut along the cross is accurate. But no institutional source found here says the 1247 design was *intended* as a cutting guide. Short Cross coins were also cut along their cross.
- **Suggested wording:** "In 1247 the cross was extended to the coin's edge so clipping would be obvious. It also gave a handy line to cut along."
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - The Royal Mint, "Medieval Voided Long Cross Silver Penny" – https://royalmint.com/shop/ancient-historic/medieval/medieval-voided-long-cross-silver-penny. Says it was introduced to replace deteriorating Short Cross; the long cross made clipped ends obvious.
  - Rod Blunt, "Long Cross pennies" – https://www.rodblunt.com/long-cross-pennies. Calls it the last type officially cut into halves and quarters; it ended in 1279.
  - Coincraft, "Henry III 1216-1272" – https://coincraft.com/henry-iii-1216-1272 (dealer). Says the cross gave guidelines for cutting into halfpennies and farthings.

## 4. "Farthing" from Old English feorthing, "a fourth part"
**Verdict: Established.**
- Etymonline: OE *feorðing* "quarter of a penny; a fourth part", a diminutive of *feorða* "fourth" + *-ing* "fractional part". It also notes the modern coin was first minted under Edward I and abolished in 1961.
- Source: Etymonline, "farthing" – https://www.etymonline.com/word/farthing (search result, not fetched).

## 5. A penny was roughly a day's wage for a labourer
**Verdict: Reasonable reconstruction (estimate). Keep it hedged.**
- Late-13th/early-14th-century daily cash rates for unskilled labourers and helpers usually run from about 1d to 1½d, sometimes 2d. Harvest work paid more, and women and boys less. A BHO Middlesex wage table (early 1300s) shows thatchers' helps and labourers at roughly 1d–2d. Many farm workers (famuli) were paid largely in kind (grain liveries), so a cash day-rate is only part of the picture (Claridge, Delabastita & Gibbs, LSE).
- **Suggested wording:** "An unskilled labourer might earn about a penny, or a penny and a half, for a day's work" (flag as an estimate). Avoid "a penny was *the* daily wage".
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - British History Online, "Social and economic history: Appendix 2" (VCH Middlesex wage table) – https://prod.british-history.ac.uk/node/15304. Shows labourer/help rates of ~1d–2d in the early 1300s.
  - LSE Research Online, Claridge, Delabastita & Gibbs, "(In-kind) wages and labour relations in the Middle Ages" – https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/125597. Says remuneration before the late 14th century was largely in kind.
  - Cambridge, Dyer, *Standards of Living in the Later Middle Ages*, ch. "Wage-earners" – https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/standards-of-living-in-the-later-middle-ages/wageearners/FD4F413E2C27CE55E7B911D0B02DD6A9. Context: at Cuxham in 1300, wage-earners made up more than half of the adult males. Dyer warns that wage series must be read in social context.

## 6. Assize of Bread and Ale (c.1266–67): loaf weight tied to grain price; fines and pillory
**Verdict: Established. Note on the date.**
- The statute (Assisa panis et cervisie) is conventionally dated 51 Henry III (1266–67), and it codified older assizes going back to Henry II/John. The price was fixed (e.g. a farthing loaf) and the **weight** varied with the price of a quarter of wheat. Example: at 12d a quarter, the farthing wastel loaf weighed £6 16s (a money-weight expression); at 18d a quarter it weighed £4 10s 8d. Penalties: amercement (fines) for a first offence, with the pillory for repeated or serious offences. Brewers who broke the ale assize faced the tumbrel.
- **Suggested wording:** "Under the Assize of Bread, a farthing loaf always cost a farthing, but its weight went up or down with the price of wheat. Short-weight bakers were fined; repeat offenders could be put in the pillory."
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - Fordham Internet Medieval Sourcebook, "Assize of Bread and Beer" – https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/breadbeer.asp. Gives the farthing wastel-loaf weight table against the wheat price, as above.
  - Wikipedia, "Assize of Bread and Ale" – https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assize_of_Bread_and_Ale (tertiary). Says the law traces to proclamations of Henry II/John; loaf weight fell as the wheat price rose; offenders could be pilloried.
  - Federation of Bakers, "History of bread – Medieval Times" – https://www.fob.uk.com/about-the-bread-industry/history-of-bread-antiquity/history-bread-medieval-times/ (trade body, popular). Mentions the pillory.

## 7. "Baker's dozen"
**Verdict: The phrase is late-16th century. The Assize link is a popular explanation (unproven).**
- The earliest citations are 1590s: Thomas Nashe, *Have with You to Saffron-Walden* (1596), is widely reported as the OED's earliest. John Cooke's *Tu Quoque* (1599/1614) is also cited. That is over three centuries after the 1266 statute. The "extra loaf to avoid short-weight penalties" story is popularised by Hotten's slang dictionary (1864) and is not documented in medieval sources.
- **Suggested wording:** If used at all, say "Centuries later, people explained the 'baker's dozen' of 13 as bakers adding an extra loaf to stay on the right side of the law – a good story, but not a proven one." Do not show a 1260 character saying "baker's dozen".
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - Phrases.org.uk, "Baker's dozen" – https://www.phrases.org.uk/meanings/bakers-dozen.html. Gives early 1599 use and questions the Assize story.
  - Wordorigins.org (Dave Wilton), "baker's dozen" – https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/bakers-dozen. Says the phrase appears long after the medieval laws, and the earliest uses aren't about baking.

## 8. Open central hearth, no chimney; cruck, wattle and daub, thatch
**Verdict: Established for ordinary peasant houses c.1260.**
- The central open hearth was the norm. Smoke escaped through the roof, sometimes via a smoke hole or louver. Chimneys spread widely only in the 16th century. Cruck framing is typical of the Midlands, North and West, but not the South-East/East Anglia, where box-frame and post construction were used. Wattle-and-daub infill and thatch (straw or reed) were standard. Note that by c.1200–1300 many peasant houses were well-built carpentered structures; Midlands survivals have been dendro-dated to the late 13th century onward (Alcock & Miles). So don't portray a flimsy hovel.
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - Current Archaeology, "Peasant houses in Midland England: How the Black Death prompted a building boom" – https://archaeology.co.uk/articles/features/peasant-houses-in-midland-england-how-the-black-death-prompted-a-building-boom.htm. Says dendro-dating shows thousands of medieval peasant houses survive in the Midlands, and cruck houses are typical.
  - Wikipedia, "Peasant homes in medieval England" – https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant_homes_in_medieval_England (tertiary, well-referenced). Covers the central hearth, no chimney, and wattle and daub with thatch.
- Background (not verified online): Dyer, "English peasant buildings in the later Middle Ages", *Medieval Archaeology* 30 (1986). Alcock & Miles, *The Medieval Peasant House in Midland England* (2013).

## 9. Longhouses; animals under the same roof; a hen inside
**Verdict: Longhouses are established for upland and western areas (Dartmoor/Hound Tor). Lowland longhouses (e.g. Wharram Percy) are debated. A hen indoors is a reasonable reconstruction.**
- English Heritage's Hound Tor page describes 13th-century stone longhouses: the family at the upper end, cattle tethered in the lower byre (shippon) in winter, with sloping ground draining waste. The Wharram Percy evidence is a matter of interpretation. Many lowland peasant houses by the 13th century had separate byres and barns around a yard (toft).
- A hen wandering in by day is plausible and harmless. Poultry were ubiquitous, and doors stood open. This is not directly documented for a specific house, so present it as everyday colour rather than a claim.
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - English Heritage, "Hound Tor Deserted Medieval Village" – https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/hound-tor-deserted-medieval-village/history. Describes 13th-century longhouses, family at one end and cattle in the byre at the other.
  - English Heritage, "Research on Wharram Percy Deserted Medieval Village" – https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/wharram-percy-deserted-medieval-village/history/research/. Says the excavated longhouse was high-status, and the lives of poorer peasants are less well understood.
  - University of Hull, "Beresford's Lost Villages" – https://dmv.wordpress.hull.ac.uk/?p=72. Says the Wharram longhouses' construction "has been a matter of interpretation".

## 10. Share of the population living in the countryside c.1300
**Verdict: Established as an estimate: roughly 80–90% rural.**
- Britnell puts towns at up to ~15% by c.1300, Dyer at ~20%, so the rural share is ~80–85%. Older and textbook figures of ~90% assume a stricter definition of "town". Recommend "about four out of five people, or more, lived in the countryside", flagged as an estimate.
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - History Today, "How Urban was Medieval England?" (Christopher Dyer) – https://www.historytoday.com/archive/how-urban-was-medieval-england. Argues for a higher urban share.
  - IHR Reviews in History, Review no. 294 – https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/294/print/. Reports Dyer's ~20% urban by 1300.
  - Cambridge Urban History of Britain, conclusion – https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/books/cambridge-urban-history-of-britain/conclusion/3895942C201A0E4FDAA8613ADD9ADD81. Reports Britnell's ~10% (1086) to ~15% (c.1300).

## 11. Villeins owed week-work, organised by the reeve
**Verdict: Established, with heavy local variation.**
- Customary tenants (especially full and half virgaters) commonly owed 2–3 days a week of week-work, more in summer and harvest (boon-works). Some owed much less or commuted it to cash. The reeve was usually an unfree tenant elected or chosen from the villagers; he oversaw the works and kept the account, under the bailiff/steward. Recommend "often two or three days a week".
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - British History Online, VCH Cambs, "Elsworth: Economic history" – https://prod.british-history.ac.uk/node/8835. Says yardlanders owed week-work each Monday and Friday plus Wednesday ploughing; croft-holders one day.
  - Merriam-Webster, "week work" – https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/week%20works. Defines it as usually 2 or 3 days, but 4 or 5 in summer.
  - Univ. of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, Martha Carlin, HIST 371 lecture outline – https://sites.uwm.edu/carlin/?p=417. Defines the reeve as a free peasant or villein overseeing the day-to-day work of the manor, supervised by the bailiff.

## 12. "Curfew" from Old French covre-feu; the William I story is legend
**Verdict: Etymology established. The William the Conqueror origin is disputed/legendary.**
- The word comes from Anglo-Norman/Old French *coeuvre-feu* / *covrefeu* "cover fire": a bell signalled fires to be covered or banked at night (the OED's earliest English evidence is c.1300). The attribution to William I comes from later chroniclers. The custom existed across medieval Europe, and the story is not supported by early sources. For 1260, an evening bell to cover fires is a reasonable town or village custom to show, but not a Norman "law".
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - OUPblog, Anatoly Liberman, "A four-forked etymology: curfew" (Feb 2024) – https://blog.oup.com/2024/02/a-four-forked-etymology-curfew/. Calls the William attribution unsustainable; the custom was pan-European.
  - Word Histories, "curfew" – https://wordhistories.net/2016/08/22/curfew/. Says the William story comes from later chroniclers "without any countenance from the early writers".

## 13. Peasant clothing c.1260; maslin, rye or barley bread
**Verdict: Clothing is a reasonable reconstruction (broadly right, with small tweaks). The bread claim is established.**
- **Men:** a knee-length wool tunic, belted, often hitched up for work; braies (linen underpants) underneath; hose or bare legs; a hood (often separate, with a shoulder cape); turnshoes or low ankle boots. Turnshoes are well attested archaeologically (e.g. Museum of London *Shoes and Pattens*), though not verified online here.
- **Women:** an ankle-length gown or tunic over a linen shift (smock). "Kirtle" is acceptable. The head was covered: a linen veil or headcloth, with the wimple common among married women in the 13th century, and an apron for work.
- **Bread:** Dyer's study of harvest workers shows 13th-century workers fed barley and rye bread (and maslin), replaced by wheat bread by the 15th century. White wheaten (wastel, later manchet) bread was elite food.
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - Medievalists.net summary of C. Dyer, "Changes in Diet in the Late Middle Ages: the Case of Harvest Workers" (*Agricultural History Review* 36, 1988) – https://www.medievalists.net/2010/10/changes-in-diet-in-the-late-middle-ages-the-case-of-harvest-workers/. Says "Barley and rye bread was replaced by wheat".
  - Wikipedia, "English medieval clothing" – https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_medieval_clothing (tertiary). Describes short belted working tunics, braies and hose, hoods, and veil and wimple.

## 14. Weekly markets granted by royal charter under Henry III
**Verdict: Established.**
- The Gazetteer (Samantha Letters, Centre for Metropolitan History, IHR) records hundreds of Henry III-era grants of a weekly market (one named day) by royal charter, often with an annual fair. Examples: Queen Camel, Somerset – a Monday market by charter of 12 Sept 1264 to John de Burgo. Horsley, Derbys. – a Thursday market by letter close, 8 Sept 1267. 1227 – the bishop of Bath was granted weekly markets in all his manors "so that it be not to the damage of neighbouring fairs or markets". Note that many villages did not have their own market; peasants walked to one within a few miles.
- Sources (search results, not fetched):
  - IHR / CMH, "Gazetteer of Markets and Fairs in England and Wales to 1516" (Samantha Letters) – https://archives.history.ac.uk/gazetteer/gazweb1.html. Index of markets and fairs.
  - IHR, "Gazetteer ... : Introduction" (Appendix II) – https://archives.history.ac.uk/gazetteer/AppII.html. Gives the 1227 general grant to the bishop of Bath.
  - British History Online, List & Index Society edition – https://www.british-history.ac.uk/list-index-soc/markets-fairs-gazetteer-to-1516. The published version of the Gazetteer.
