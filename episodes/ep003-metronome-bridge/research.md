# Research record — Episode 003

**Episode:** ep003-metronome-bridge — *The Viral Metronome Trick That Shut a London Bridge*
**Viral source:** Ikeguchi Laboratory (Saitama University), "Metronome synchronization (32 pieces)", YouTube, September 2012. Widely shared; one undated source cites 4.4 million views, which could not be verified, so the episode does not state a view count. The episode recreates the effect in original animation and does not use the footage.
**Central question:** Why do 32 metronomes started at random end up ticking together, and what does that have to do with a bridge that closed two days after opening?
**Why it matters:** Spontaneous synchronisation shows up in fireflies, heart cells, power grids and crowds. The Millennium Bridge is the most famous case of a structure being pushed by its own users, and the science is still argued about.
**Proposed answer:** A movable base couples the metronomes: each swing pushes the base, and the base pushes the others, until they lock in step. On the bridge, the deck's sideways motion and pedestrians' balancing steps fed each other. Above a critical crowd size the wobble grew sharply. Whether the crowd actually fell into step, or just balancing was enough, is debated.

## Uncertainties and limitations
- The animated chart is qualitative; it does not plot measured data.
- "About 160" comes from Strogatz et al.'s model as reported by Cornell; Arup's own threshold figure was not confirmed from an accessible source.
- The bridge's lateral modes and walking frequencies are deliberately left out to keep it accurate and short.
