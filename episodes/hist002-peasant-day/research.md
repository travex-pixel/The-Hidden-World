## 1. Boon-works (precariae, "bedrips"), wet and dry boons, and what the lord fed reapers

**Verdict: established.** Boon-works were extra harvest days owed by the whole household, usually with the lord providing food. **The ale/dry distinction is established** in the records, but **"wet boon" itself is not attested** in what I found. The documented forms are *ale bederep* / "ale" day, *sec* (dry) *bederep*, a "water" day, and Latin *precariae siccae* vs *non siccae*. The specific menus are **established in principle but vary by manor**. The exact figures below need checking against the cited texts.

**Best accurate wording:**
> "At harvest the lord could summon his tenants, and often their entire households, for extra 'boon' days of reaping, called *precariae* in Latin and 'bedrips' in English. In return he fed them. The records distinguish 'ale' boons, where drink was served, from 'dry' ones where it wasn't."

Key points and sources:

- **Term and meaning.** The *Anglo-Norman Dictionary* entry **bederep** (earliest citation 1226) defines it as a reaping service at the lord's request. It quotes *"Debent venire in autumpno ad precariam quæ vocatur a le bederepe"* ("they must come in autumn to the boon called the bederepe"). A second sense, labelled **"ale, sec bederep"**, is a bederep "in exchange for the day's provision of food, with or without beer/cider". It cites "ale bedereps" in 1298. *Sec* = dry. This is the strongest evidence that a with-drink/without-drink distinction existed. (Snippet-only.)
- **Latin terms.** A manorial-language glossary (Ole Miss) says that on some *precariae* the lord supplied food but no drink, and these "were therefore called **siccae**". Days with both food and drink were **non siccae**. This is a secondary glossary. (Snippet-only.)
- **"Water" vs "ale" day.** The Ruislip, Northwood & Eastcote Local History Society *Journal* (2021) glosses the Ruislip custumal's *benerthes* as boondays (*precariae*). It says the harvest *bedrepe* was called a **"water" or "ale" day depending on the fare provided**. The Ruislip custumal is dated no later than 1248 (Morris, LAMAS vol. 19), which is close to your period. (Snippet-only.)
- **Whole household.** Jean Birrell, "Peasants eating and drinking", *Agricultural History Review* 63:1 (2015), pp. 1–18. Snippets say boon works were concentrated at the hay and corn harvests and demanded of **"the tenants' whole households", meaning families, servants and farmworkers**. Custumals quoted: **Horninglow** (Staffs) has the tenant come with his whole *familia* **except his wife**. **Houghton** (Hunts) says the same. **Alveston** (Warks) excepts **his wife and his shepherd**. Birrell's abstract says peasants could tell good food and drink from bad, valued orderly service ("dining"), and **bargained with lords over what they were owed**. Some custumals call these services **"love boons"** to frame them as voluntary.
- **No reaping your own field that day.** The Battle Abbey custumals (Camden Society, 1887, covering 1283–1312) are reported in a Google Books snippet to require some tenants to bring their whole households to the **Great Precaria** in autumn. The same snippet says **no one could reap on that day except in the lord's field**. (Snippet-only. The date is later than 1260.)
- **Fed twice a day.** VCH Wiltshire iv, "Medieval agriculture", describes 13th-century manors of the Bishopric of Winchester. Reapers had to cut ½ acre a day, **"each reaper taking 1 sheaf for himself"**. At Bishopstone a 3-day *midrepe* was owed, plus 3 *precaria* by larger customary tenants, who sent 2 men and **"were then fed twice in the day"**. (Snippet-only.)
- **Menus.**
  - Birrell (snippet via search summary) mentions **bread, bacon and cheese** at an Essex autumn boon and **cheese and ale** for reapers at the great wheat boon at Denton (Sussex). She also notes custumals stressed moderation, e.g. "bread and relish". *Medium confidence: these details came from a search-engine summary of the AGHR volume PDF and should be checked in the article.*
  - Blount's *Fragmenta antiquitatis* (1679 antiquarian collection of tenures) records a custom of one boon-day a week in harvest, **"when every two workmen had three Boon-loaves with Companage allowed them"**. *Companage* means food eaten with the bread. Manor and date are not given in the snippet, so do not date it to 1260.
  - A search summary attributed to Gies, *Life in a Medieval Village*, says that at **Elton (1298)** about 250 boon workers on the second day ate bread from 11 "rings" of grain, **800 herrings, 7d-worth of salt cod and 5 cheeses**. **UNVERIFIED.** I could not corroborate it in a second search. Check Ratcliff (ed.), *Elton Manorial Records 1279–1351* (Roxburghe Club, 1946) before using any number.
- **Ramsey rule.** A search summary of a Ramsey custumal says the boon day ran only until *none* (mid-afternoon) if no food was supplied. If the lord wanted a longer day he had to provide dinner. (Snippet-only, medium confidence.)

**Suggested safe on-screen detail:** "Bread, cheese and, on a good day, ale." Avoid specific herring counts until checked.

## 2. Week-work, the reeve and tally sticks

**Verdicts:**
- Week-work of roughly 2–3 days a week, heavier at harvest: **established as a general pattern**, but it varied hugely between manors and some services had already been commuted to money.
- Reeve usually a villein, chosen or elected from the tenants: **established**. Some reeves were elected by the villagers and others appointed by the lord.
- Tallies used in manorial accounting: **established**.

**Best accurate wording:**
> "A typical villein owed the lord a set number of days' work each week, often two or three, and more at harvest. It was usually organised by the reeve, himself a villager chosen from among the tenants for a year at a time, who kept count partly with notched wooden tallies."

- **Elton (Hunts).** Gies, *Life in a Medieval Village*, "The Village at Work" (erenow mirror). A virgater owed **2 days a week, rising to 3 a week from 1 August to 8 September**. The snippet mentions further "after autumn works", totalling 31½ days of autumn and post-autumn works. The same chapter says all able-bodied adults, **women and children included**, turned out for reaping, binding, carrying and gleaning at the harvest boon, "except those too old or weak". (Snippet-only.)
- **Somerford (Wilts).** VCH Wiltshire iv: virgaters "worked for 3 days in the week with 1 man". (Snippet-only.)
- **Reeve elected.** The *Seneschaucy* (anonymous estate-management treatise, usually dated early 1270s, close to 1260), in Lamond's translation via the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook and Medievalists.net, says: **"The reeve ought to be elected and presented by the common consent of the township, as the best husbandman..."** It adds that no reeve should stay more than a year unless proven "most profitable and faithful". Oschinsky's 1971 edition is the scholarly text. (Snippet-only.)
- **Reeve's status.** Wikipedia "Reeve (England)" says the reeve was usually a peasant chosen yearly, generally at Michaelmas. He was appointed by the lord on some manors and elected by the peasants on others. A glossary snippet says he was "usually elected" by the peasants and was a villein. For the script, say "usually a villein".
- **Tallies.**
  - *Seneschaucy*: "Each reeve ought every year to account with his bailiff, and **tally** the works and customs commuted in the manor." The hayward should "**tally with the reeve all the seed, boon-work, customs, and labor**" owed in the year.
  - The *Hosbondrie*, as quoted by Tanja Skambraks in an OAPEN open-access chapter on tally sticks, says the reeve should not buy, sell, receive or deliver anything "**unless by tally and good witness**".
  - Tallies were split notched sticks, with each party keeping half. They were used alongside written account rolls, not instead of them.

## 3. Suit of mill, multure and hand-mills (querns)

**Verdicts:**
- Obligation to grind at the lord's mill and pay multure: **established**.
- Typical rate: **established that it varied**. About 1/16 is often cited as most common, and 1/13 applied to unfree tenants in the north. **1/24 is not documented** as a norm in what I found; Langdon used it only as a modelling bound.
- Hand-mills forbidden or licensed on some manors: **established** (St Albans Abbey manors).
- Hand-mills confiscated: **established for St Albans**. I found no other English example.

**Best accurate wording:**
> "Many tenants were bound by 'suit of mill': they had to grind their grain at the lord's mill and hand over a cut of it, the multure, often around a sixteenth. Some lords banned or licensed hand-mills at home so nobody could dodge the toll."

- **Multure rates.**
  - Review of John Langdon, *Mills in the Medieval Economy: England 1300–1540* (OUP 2004), in *The Medieval Review*: "1/16 has often been considered by historians as the most common rate". The review says rates ranged from about 1/13 (far north) to lower fractions in the competitive south. Langdon models throughput at 1/16, 1/20 and 1/24, but that is not evidence that 1/24 was a norm.
  - Langdon, "Lordship and peasant consumerism in the milling industry of early fourteenth-century England", *Past & Present* 145 (1994): "one-thirteenth seems to have been the multure rate that applied to unfree tenants throughout the north", citing Holt, *Mills of Medieval England*, pp. 80–1. He also says evasion of suit of mill, through other lords' mills or hand-mills, was substantial. (Snippet-only.)
- **Hand-mills banned or licensed.** Rebecca Toepfer, International Medieval Congress, Leeds, 2016, session 514: the abbot of St Albans "sought to ensure that his tenants were only using mills belonging to the abbey, **banning the use of hand mills on his manors**". The evidence comes mainly from Codicote, where some tenants held **licences** for hand-mills. This was a factor in the 1327 and 1381 revolts. (Snippet-only, conference abstract.)
- **Court case.** UK Flour Millers, "A new economy" (snippet): in **1243** John of the Mills charged Martin of Feltham and Agnes of Staines, his wife, with using a hand-mill and so denying him multure. The couple **won** and could keep their hand-mill. This shows enforcement was contested. (Snippet-only. The site is a trade-body history page, not a primary edition.)
- **Archaeology.** Ben Jervis, "Becoming Through Milling", *Cambridge Archaeological Journal* 32:2 (2022): domestic hand-milling persisted in medieval Kent alongside mechanised mills. (Abstract only.)
- **Do not import Scottish practice.** Wikipedia "Thirlage" says that in Scotland querns were broken to enforce mill suit. Don't present that as English practice.

## 4. HEADLINE FACT: the St Albans millstone floor (1331, then 1381)

**Verdict: established as reported by a contemporary chronicler.** The core is solid: the abbey took townspeople's hand-mills and set the stones into the parlour floor, and the 1381 rebels levered them up, smashed them and shared out the pieces in a deliberate echo of the Sunday "blessed bread". The source is Thomas Walsingham, a St Albans monk writing in the *Gesta Abbatum* and *Chronica maiora*. He is hostile to the rebels, but he is an insider and a contemporary of 1381. Several popular details are **inaccurate or uncertain** and are flagged below.

### What the sources support

1. **Background.** Townspeople repeatedly defied the abbey's mill monopoly: they refused the abbey mills in 1274, 1314 and 1326–27 (1381.online "Causes cards"). In 1274 they "set up handmills in their own houses" (Galbraith, *The Abbey of St Albans from 1300 to the Dissolution*, 1911, Project Gutenberg). In 1313–14 judges again decided against the townsmen's claim to hand-mills (snippet of VCH Herts on British History Online). **The 1274 dispute is the closest to your 1260 setting.**
2. **Who and when.** The abbot was **Richard of Wallingford**, the astronomer and clockmaker. He became abbot in 1327 and died in 1336; some older sources give 1326–1335. After the townsmen won a ruling in 1327, the abbey got it overturned. In **1331** the townspeople were made to surrender their hand-mills (1381.online "Causes cards"; Nicholas Whyte's essay; St Albans Cathedral brochure "Richard of Wallingford, 1327"). The cathedral brochure says they "surrendered their hand mills which he used to **pave the parlour floor**". Whyte says he "cemented the grinding stones into the Abbey's parlour floor, where the monks daily trampled over them" but treats the episode cautiously ("if it happened"). John North's biography *God's Clockmaker* covers Richard's mills; I did not see its text.
3. **What Walsingham actually says about the stones.** Snippet translations put them in the floor of the **parlour doorway / entrance of the outer parlour**, laid there **"as a record and memorial of an earlier dispute"**. Another snippet says "a memorial of an old agreement between the villeins and the monastery in the time of Abbot Richard". So it was **not necessarily a whole floor paved with millstones**. "Paved the parlour" is the popular phrasing.
4. **1381.** On **15 June 1381**, during the Peasants' Revolt, townsmen led by William Grindecobbe entered the cloister and "lifted up the millstones" from the parlour entrance. Walsingham reports they took them to the commons and **smashed them into small pieces, "giving a part of them to each man, as the bread that has been blessed is distributed and bestowed upon the Lord's people in the parochial churches."** That translation is quoted from Walsingham's *St Albans Chronicle* (ed. Taylor, Childs & Watkiss, OUP 2003, pp. 457–9) in "Introduction: people, places and possessions in late medieval England", *Journal of Medieval History* (2019). Snippet-only.
   - Steven Justice, *Writing and Rebellion: England in 1381* (UC Press, 1994), connects it to **Corpus Christi**: the act came two days after the feast. Margaret Aston is cited as tracing a chain from milling to mill-stones to holy bread to communion.
   - A 2025 article, R. M. Gammie, "Memory, materiality, and trauma: revisiting the parlour floor, St. Albans, 1381", *Rethinking History* 29:2, reads the act as material memory of centuries of suit-of-mill grievance.
5. **Aftermath.** Once the revolt collapsed, Walsingham says the stones were brought back. Gammie quotes him: "about the time of vespers the **six** stolen millstones were brought back" and set where the others had been. One search summary dates the return to 12 July 1381; that date is unverified. Gammie notes it is **unclear how many stones were involved** in total. Grindecobbe and others were hanged (stalbanshistory.org).

### Corrections and uncertainties to respect

- **"Holy bread" ≠ communion.** The comparison is to the **blessed bread (*pain bénit*, the "holy loaf") shared out after Sunday mass in parish churches**, not the consecrated Host. Popular summaries saying "like communion wafers" overstate it. Safe wording: "shared out like the blessed bread handed round in church on Sundays".
- **Two different Richards of Wallingford.** The *abbot* (d. 1336) confiscated the mills. A *different* Richard of Wallingford, a townsman and later constable of Wallingford Castle (Wikipedia, "Richard of Wallingford (constable)"), was active in the 1381 rising and brought a royal letter to St Albans. Don't conflate them. Gammie explicitly sets the constable aside.
- **The abbot in 1381** was **Thomas de la Mare** (abbot 1349–96), not Richard.
- **Conflicting popular versions.**
  - The 1381.online chronology says the stones were "dug up and returned to the families from whom they had been taken". The CHR "And Did Those Feet" blog says the rebels "took them home".
  - Walsingham, as quoted in the scholarship, says they were **smashed and the pieces handed round**, and that some stones were later returned.
  - Redbournbury Mill's website wrongly puts the confiscation in 1381 and the stones in the **Chapter House**.
  - Prefer the Walsingham-based account.
- **"Querns" vs "millstones".** The confiscated objects were the townspeople's **hand-mills**, i.e. querns. Walsingham and the modern literature call the stones in the floor "millstones" / "stones of hand-mills". Either is fine. "Hand-mill stones" is the most precise.
- **Not a 1260 village event.** St Albans was a town under abbatial lordship. Use the story as a flash-forward ("seventy years later, at St Albans...").

**Best accurate wording (narration):**
> "At St Albans, an abbot confiscated the townspeople's hand-mills and set the stones into the floor at the entrance of his parlour, a permanent reminder of who controlled the grinding of grain. Fifty years later, in the revolt of 1381, the townsfolk prised them up, smashed them, and handed round the pieces the way blessed bread was shared out in church on Sundays."

**Title-safe wording:** "The abbot who paved his floor with peasants' millstones" / "Medieval rebels shared smashed millstones like holy bread". Use "blessed bread" in narration.

## 5. Heriot (lord) and mortuary (church)

**Verdict: established.** The "best beast" to the lord and "second-best" to the church is the classic pattern, but **customs varied**. Some manors took money instead, some took the best chattel, and some thresholds applied.

**Best accurate wording:**
> "When a villein died, his lord could claim a death-duty called heriot, often his best animal, and the parish priest could claim a 'mortuary', often the second-best."

- University of Nottingham Manuscripts & Special Collections, manorial glossary: heriot = "a payment made on the death of a tenant". (Snippet.)
- Oxford Research Archive article (title not visible in snippet): heriot was paid by tenants to lords at death, "**traditionally in the form of their best beast**". It could sometimes be a money sum fixed in advance. (Snippet.)
- Ole Miss feudal and manorial glossaries: heriot is "often... the dead man's best beast or chattel". **Mortuary** is "the death duty paid by villein to parish priest... usually **second-best beast** or chattel". (Snippet.)
- Blackstone, *Commentaries* (LONANG edition): the lord's heriot was taken first, then the second-best animal went to the church. Mortuaries were "a customary gift claimed by and due to the minister in very many parishes". This is 18th-century legal commentary describing older custom. (Snippet.)
- A modern blog says a mortuary applied only if the deceased owned 3+ beasts. That is a local detail; don't generalise it.

## 6. Merchet and leyrwite

**Verdicts:**
- Merchet as a fine paid to the lord for a daughter's marriage: **established**. Its purpose is debated. On some manors it was also levied on men (Bredfield).
- Leyrwite as a fine for illicit sex: **established**. It fell overwhelmingly on unfree women, especially the poor. It peaked c.1250–1350, which **fits 1260**, and existed only in England, in some regions.

**Best accurate wording:**
> "An unfree father could owe the lord a fine, merchet, when his daughter married. And on many manors an unfree woman who had sex outside marriage could be fined 'leyrwite'. The fine almost always landed on the woman, not the man."

- University of Nottingham manorial glossary: merchet = "a payment made for obtaining permission for a daughter to marry". (Snippet.)
- Judith M. Bennett, "Writing Fornication: Medieval Leyrwite and its Historians", *Transactions of the Royal Historical Society* 6th ser. 13 (2003), pp. 131–62. The abstract (Cambridge Core / Medievalists.net) describes leyrwite as a fine for fornication levied on bondwomen. It flourished especially c.1250–1350, only in England and only in some regions. It was directed especially at the poor, and more at bastardy than fornication. Bennett argues it was one means of regulating poor women and their families. (Snippet.)
- "The Relative Weakness of Serfdom and Lordship in Later Medieval England: The Example of Bredfield (Suffolk)", *Jahrbuch für Wirtschaftsgeschichte* (de Gruyter, 2026). It summarises the merchet debate:
  - Searle: a tax on dowries.
  - Faith: a charge proving villein status.
  - Brand & Hyams: an irregular windfall levied on the more prosperous families.
  - At Bredfield both males and females were liable. (Snippet.)
- **Avoid:** do not link merchet to the *ius primae noctis* / "droit du seigneur". There is no good evidence for that in medieval England, and merchet is the real, duller fact. **[background knowledge; the myth was not searched in detail]**

## 7. Gleaning, sickles, sheaves and stooks, women reaping

**Verdicts:**
- Gleaning by the poor after harvest: **established**. By-laws regulated it, and the able-bodied were sometimes barred if they could earn wages reaping. Most specific by-laws I found are 14th–15th century.
- Reaping by sickle: **established**.
- Sheaves bound and set up in stooks/shocks: **established practice** **[term "stook" not confirmed in a source this session]**.
- Women reaping: **established**. They appear in the Luttrell Psalter, and Gies says women and children turned out for harvest boons.

**Best accurate wording:**
> "Reapers cut the corn by hand with sickles. Men, women and older children worked the harvest, binding the stalks into sheaves. Afterwards, the poorest villagers were allowed to glean the leftover ears, under rules the village set."

- Gleaning rules:
  - Leicestershire & Rutland local history blog, "The early fifteenth-century Wymeswold bye-laws revisited" (2024), drawing on W. O. Ault: at **Islip in 1407** "peasants who earn a penny a day will have their rights of gleaning limited". At **Wymeswold** the threshold was 2d a day.
  - The blog describes gleaning as a customary right of "the poorest (often landless)".
  - Ault's article "By-laws of gleaning and the problems of harvest" (*Economic History Review*, 1961) and his *Open-Field Husbandry and the Village Community* (1965) are the scholarly basis. I saw titles only.
  - A Broughton 1286 gleaning by-law appeared in one summary but could **not** be confirmed. Don't use it.
- Sickle: Museum of English Rural Life (Reading) catalogue entry for a sickle: "the earliest tool for reaping corn... in common use until 1870". "The reaper stooped and, grasping a bunch of corn in one hand, curved the sickle round it and cut the straw." (Snippet.)
- Each Winchester reaper kept **1 sheaf for himself** (VCH Wilts iv, see §1). That makes a nice on-screen perk.
- Women reaping:
  - The Luttrell Psalter (British Library Add MS 42130, c.1320–40), **f. 172v**, is titled by the BL "**Female Labourers Bringing in the Harvest**" (BL shop listing).
  - Other reports describe women reaping with sickles and a man binding sheaves. I did not confirm the tools on the folio myself.
  - The Psalter is ~70 years after 1260. It is fine as a visual reference, but say "a 14th-century manuscript".

## 8. Harvest timing (Lammas, 1 August)

**Verdict: established.** The corn harvest ran from about **1 August (Lammas) into September**. Elton's "autumn works" period ran **1 August–8 September**.

**Best accurate wording:**
> "The grain harvest began around Lammas, 1 August, the 'loaf-mass', when bread from the first wheat was blessed. It ran into September."

- Wikipedia, "Lammas": Old English *hlāf-mæsse*, "loaf-mass", on 1 August. It was probably the day loaves from the first wheat harvest were blessed in church. Lammas also marks the end of haymaking. (Snippet.)
- Gies (Elton): week-work rose "from August 1 to September 8" (see §2).
- Michaelmas (29 Sept) closed the manorial accounting year. Its role as the formal end of harvest is **not confirmed** in my sources.

## 9. Harvest diet: pottage, bread, ale (not beer)

**Verdicts:**
- Harvest workers' diet of mostly bread, some cheese, modest ale, little meat or fish in the 13th century: **established** (Dyer).
- Bread grains barley/rye, often mixed: **established** for harvest workers (Dyer). The term **"maslin"** is **[background knowledge, not verified this session]**.
- Pottage as a staple: **reasonable reconstruction** **[background knowledge; not verified with a source this session]**.
- "Ale, not beer": **correct for 1260** **[background knowledge, not verified this session]**. Hopped beer reached England from the Low Countries in the later 14th–15th century; in 1260 the drink was unhopped ale. Cider also appears in harvest provisions (Dyer, Merton).

**Best accurate wording:**
> "Harvest food was mostly bread, coarse barley or rye loaves, with cheese and a little bacon or fish, washed down with ale. Not beer: hopped beer only caught on in England much later."

- Christopher Dyer, "Changes in diet in the late Middle Ages: the case of harvest workers", *Agricultural History Review* 36 (1988), reprinted in Dyer, *Everyday Life in Medieval England*.
  - Abstract via Medievalists.net: in the 13th century harvest workers' meals leaned heavily on bread, with some cheese and modest amounts of ale, fish and meat.
  - Over the 14th century "barley and rye bread was replaced by wheat, bacon by beef, and cider by ale". (Snippet.)
- **Sedgeford (Norfolk), 1256** is an exact period match.
  - Secondary summaries of Dyer's tables (History Skills page; search excerpts from *Everyday Life*): bread was mostly barley, and "wheat was under 8% of the bread corn, and it probably went to the supervisors".
  - The ale ration was about **2.8 pints per person per day**, with "an ounce or two of meat, mostly bacon" per 2 lb of bread.
  - By 1424 it was about 6.4 pints of ale and all-wheat bread.
  - These were the lord's harvest workforce, not necessarily boon workers. Verify figures in Dyer before quoting.
- Boon meals (Birrell, §1) also show bread, cheese, bacon and ale.

## Best 5 absurd-but-true facts for a title or thumbnail (ranked)

1. **The millstone floor.** *"An abbot confiscated his tenants' hand-mills and set the stones in his parlour floor. In 1381 rebels smashed them and shared the pieces like holy bread."*
   - Thumbnail: "PAVED WITH PEASANTS' MILLSTONES".
   - Caveats: it was St Albans, 1331/1381, and the stones were at the parlour entrance. Use "blessed bread" in narration. Present it as a flash-forward from 1260.
2. **It was illegal to grind your own flour.** *"Many peasants had to grind grain at the lord's mill, and pay him a cut. Some lords banned hand-mills at home."* (Suit of mill; St Albans Abbey's hand-mill ban and licences; multure often about 1/16 and 1/13 in the north.) Avoid "everywhere" and avoid "1/24".
3. **Your lord got your best cow when you died, and the priest got the second-best.** (Heriot plus mortuary.) Say "often" or "usually"; customs varied.
4. **"Ale" boons and "dry" boons.** *"Peasants had to drop everything to reap the lord's corn, and the records note whether the lord served ale or left it 'dry'."* (Anglo-Norman *ale / sec bederep*; Latin *precariae siccae*; Ruislip's "water" vs "ale" days.) Say "dry boon", not "wet boon"; "wet boon" isn't attested in what I found.
5. **Fined for your love life.** *"Unfree women could be fined 'leyrwite' for sex outside marriage, and fathers paid 'merchet' for a daughter's wedding."* Leyrwite fell almost entirely on women (Bennett). **Do not** mention *droit du seigneur*.

Honourable mentions:
- **Compulsory drinking parties you paid to attend** (Glastonbury scotales, 1235–61). Great for 1260 and arguably more absurd than #5.
- **No reaping your own corn on the Great Boon day** (Battle Abbey).
- **Rent paid in hens** (Merton).
