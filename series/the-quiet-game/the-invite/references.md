# References and research notes: "The Invite"

The story is fiction; the psychology is real. Citations were checked on 2026-10-12 against bibliographic records, PubMed abstracts, quoted passages and reputable secondary sources. Full papers could not be opened from the research environment. Anything unconfirmed is marked **unverified**. All effects are **tendencies, not rules**, and most come from short lab studies with student samples.

## 1. Ostracism and Cyberball (Chapter 5, "Why it hurts")
- **Williams, K. D., Cheung, C. K. T., & Choi, W. (2000).** Cyberostracism: Effects of being ignored over the Internet. *JPSP, 79*(5), 748–762. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.748 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11079239/
  - Online participants played a ball-toss game with two "players" who were computer-controlled. The more they were left out, the worse they felt and the less they felt they belonged (abstract). In Study 2, excluded participants conformed more on a later task.
  - Study 1's sample of 1,486 people from 62 countries comes from secondary summaries. The abstract does not mention self-esteem for this paper, so the episode doesn't claim it.
- **Zadro, L., Williams, K. D., & Richardson, R. (2004).** How low can you go? Ostracism by a computer is sufficient to lower self-reported levels of belonging, control, self-esteem, and meaningful existence. *JESP, 40*(4), 560–567. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222425729
  - People told the other players were a computer still reported lower belonging after being excluded. Study 2's design details and the DOI are **unverified**.
- **Williams, K. D. (2007).** Ostracism. *Annual Review of Psychology, 58*, 425–452. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.58.110405.085641. This review argues that people detect exclusion quickly and painfully because spotting it helped them survive.
- **Caveat:** Hartgerink et al. (2015), a meta-analysis of 120 Cyberball studies (N = 11,869), found a large average effect. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0127002 These are short games with feelings measured straight afterwards, so they don't show how big the effect is in an office.
- **Narrated:**
  - "In one well-known experiment, people played a simple online game of catch with two strangers."
  - "Being left out made people feel worse, and less like they belonged…"
  - "In a later study, it still stung when people were told the other players were just a computer."
  - "…people who had been left out were more likely to go along with the group afterwards."

## 2. Ostracism at work (Chapter 5)
- **O'Reilly, J., Robinson, S. L., Berdahl, J. L., & Banki, S. (2015).** Is negative attention better than no attention? The comparative effects of ostracism and harassment at work. *Organization Science, 26*(3), 774–793. https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2014.0900
  - Employees rated ostracism as more socially acceptable and less harmful than harassment.
  - Ostracism was more strongly linked to low belonging and worse well-being.
  - Ostracism, but not harassment, predicted people actually leaving about three years later.
  - **Caveats:** correlational and self-reported. The evidence that belonging explains the link to quitting was weak (p < .10).
- **Narrated:** "But one study found it was more strongly linked to feeling you don't belong, and to people leaving their jobs years later."

## 3. Email tone (Chapter 5, "Why it hurts")
- **Kruger, J., Epley, N., Parker, J., & Ng, Z.-W. (2005).** Egocentrism over e-mail: Can we communicate as well as we think? *JPSP, 89*(6), 925–936. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16393025/
  - **Study 2** (29 pairs, email vs. voice): senders predicted about 78% accuracy. Email readers managed about 56%, which the paper calls indistinguishable from chance. Voice listeners managed about 73%.
  - The 78/56 figures come from secondary sources (NYU Stern, Psychology Today); the chance wording is the paper's. The bars on screen are labelled with the study, and "coin flip" marks the 50% line.
  - Study 1 found much higher accuracy (97% expected, 84% achieved). The episode cites Study 2 only and doesn't claim email can't carry tone.
  - **Caveats:** small student samples, only sarcastic vs. serious messages, 2005-era email with no emoji, and no direct replication found (**unverified**).
- **Byron, K. (2008).** Carrying too heavy a load? The communication and miscommunication of emotion by email. *Academy of Management Review, 33*(2), 309–327. https://doi.org/10.5465/AMR.2008.31193163
  - Argues that work emails tend to be read as more negative than the sender intended. This is a **theory paper, not an experiment**. It underpins "when we're worried, we fill it in darker", which the episode presents as a tendency.
- **Narrated:** "…expected readers to catch their tone, serious or sarcastic, about seventy-eight percent of the time. The readers got it right about fifty-six percent of the time. Barely better than a coin flip."

## 4. Spotlight effect (Chapter 5)
- **Gilovich, T., Medvec, V. H., & Savitsky, K. (2000).** The spotlight effect in social judgment. *JPSP, 78*(2), 211–222. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211
  - Students wore an embarrassing T-shirt into a room. They guessed about half the room noticed; about a quarter did.
  - Secondary sources give figures between 46% vs. 23% and "closer to 20 percent". The exact numbers are **unverified** against the paper, so the narration says "about".
  - **Caveats:** small Cornell samples. The study is about being noticed, not about anyone noticing someone's absence. Applying it to Daniel is an extrapolation, and the narration says so: "the facts are thinner than the story."

## 5. Asking for help (Chapter 6, "The question")
- **Flynn, F. J., & Lake, V. K. B. (2008).** If you need help, just ask: Underestimating compliance with direct requests for help. *JPSP, 95*(1), 128–143. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.1.128 (Lake now publishes as Vanessa Bohns.)
  - People expected to need about twice as many asks as it took. Study 6 suggests they focus on the cost of helping and overlook how awkward it is to say no.
  - **Caveats:** the requests were to strangers and the samples were mostly students. Whether the result has been replicated is unconfirmed.
- **Narrated:** "In a series of studies, people asking strangers for help expected to need about twice as many asks as it actually took."

## Deliberately left out
- **"Rejection hurts like physical pain."** Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams (2003), *Science, 302*, 290–292, had 13 participants. Later work found the brain patterns for physical pain and rejection are distinct (Woo et al., 2014, *Nature Communications*). A 53-study meta-analysis found no reliable dorsal ACC activity (Mwilambwe-Tshilobo & Spreng, 2021, *NeuroImage*). The question is still debated, so the episode makes no brain claims.

## What the episode deliberately does not do
- It doesn't say Graham excluded Daniel on purpose. His answers "fit both" readings, and the episode never settles it.
- It doesn't tell viewers their suspicions are wrong. The studies show people *tend* to over-read short emails, overestimate how much others notice them, and underestimate how willing others are to help.
- It invents no statistics. Company figures (late deliveries "down 38") are fictional and consistent with Episode 2, and `tests/test_quiet_game.py` checks them.
