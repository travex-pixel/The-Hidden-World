== 2:07  WHY IT HURTS ==

[2:08] NARRATOR: Why does a missing calendar invite feel like a punch? Psychologists have spent decades on exactly that question.
[2:15] NARRATOR: In one well-known experiment, people played a simple online game of catch with two strangers.
[2:21] NARRATOR: After a few throws, the others quietly stopped passing them the ball.
[2:26] NARRATOR: Being left out made people feel worse, and less like they belonged, even in a game with strangers they would never meet.
[2:33] NARRATOR: In a later study, it still stung when people were told the other players were just a computer.
[2:39] NARRATOR: And in the same research, people who had been left out were more likely to go along with the group afterwards. Exclusion makes us try harder to fit back in.
[2:49] NARRATOR: At work, being ignored is often treated as harmless, nothing like being shouted at.
[2:55] NARRATOR: But one study found it was more strongly linked to feeling you don't belong, and to people leaving their jobs years later.
[3:02] NARRATOR: Then there's the word itself.
[3:05] NARRATOR: In one study, people sending emails expected readers to catch their tone, serious or sarcastic, about seventy-eight percent of the time.
[3:14] NARRATOR: The readers got it right about fifty-six percent of the time. Barely better than a coin flip.
[3:21] NARRATOR: Without a voice or a face, the reader fills in the tone. And when we're worried, we fill it in darker.
[3:28] NARRATOR: And that empty chair? Probably fewer people noticed it than Daniel thought.
[3:33] NARRATOR: Students made to wear an embarrassing T-shirt into a room guessed that about half the people there noticed. About a quarter actually did.
[3:42] NARRATOR: We are the main character in our own heads. Everyone else is busy being the main character in theirs.
[3:49] NARRATOR: None of this proved Daniel wrong. Maybe he really was being frozen out.
[3:54] NARRATOR: But the facts were much thinner than the story, and the story was keeping him awake.
[3:59] NARRATOR: There was only one way to find out which one was true.

== 4:03  THE QUESTION ==

[4:03] NARRATOR: On Tuesday morning, Daniel had two options. Keep polishing the story, or ask a question.
[4:10] NARRATOR: He started with the person closest to the facts.
[4:14] DANIEL: Can I ask you something, without it being weird? Was I taken off the Ostrel call on purpose?
[4:21] LEAH: Graham set the list. He said you'd want a break from Ostrel, after everything.
[4:26] LEAH: I assumed you'd asked for it.
[4:29] DANIEL: I didn't.
[4:31] LEAH: Then ask to come back. I want you in that room.
[4:35] LEAH: For what it's worth, I noticed you weren't there. I don't think anyone else did.
[4:42] NARRATOR: Then he wrote to Graham.
[4:45] NARRATOR: A fact. A request. And an open door, in case there was a reason he didn't know.
[4:51] NARRATOR: No accusation anywhere in it.
[4:55] GRAHAM: Daniel. I took you off to lighten the load. I thought you'd earned a breather.
[5:00] DANIEL: I appreciate that. I'd rather decide my own load.
[5:05] GRAHAM: Fair enough. I'll add you back.
[5:10] NARRATOR: Was it kindness? Or a quiet way of moving Daniel to the edge of the room? Graham's answer fitted both.
[5:16] NARRATOR: And Daniel didn't need to know which. He only needed to be back in the room.
[5:22] NARRATOR: Here's the part most of us get wrong. In a series of studies, people asking strangers for help expected to need about twice as many asks as it actually took.
[5:33] NARRATOR: We focus on what helping costs the other person, and forget how awkward it is to say no to a direct, polite request.
[5:41] DANIEL: Ruth, could I have fifteen minutes with you once a month? I'd like to know what you need from me.
[5:48] RUTH: Of course. I was wondering when you'd ask.
[5:51] RUTH: And if something changes and you don't know why, come and ask me first.
