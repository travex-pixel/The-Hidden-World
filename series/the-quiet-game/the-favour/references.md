# References and research notes: "The Favour"

The story is fiction: Halvern Logistics, Ostrel Foods and every character are invented. The psychology is real. Citations were checked against bibliographic records and reputable secondary sources on 2026-10-11. The original PDFs were not read directly, because publisher pages could not be fetched from the research environment. Anything not confirmed is marked **unverified**. All effects below are **tendencies, not rules**. Many classic studies used small samples of US undergraduates.

## 1. Reciprocity (Chapter 4, "Force 1"; the ledger)
- **Gouldner, A. W. (1960).** The norm of reciprocity: A preliminary statement. *American Sociological Review, 25*(2), 161–178. https://doi.org/10.2307/2092623
  - A theory essay, not an experiment. It argues that a near-universal moral norm says people should help, and not harm, those who have helped them.
- **Regan, D. T. (1971).** Effects of a favor and liking on compliance. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 7*(6), 627–639. https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031(71)90025-4 (DOI format standard; resolution not confirmed)
  - Participants believed they were in an art-rating study. A confederate ("Joe") either brought them an unrequested soft drink or nothing, then asked them to buy raffle tickets. Favour recipients bought **about twice as many tickets**. Liking predicted purchases mainly when no favour was given.
  - Caveat: later work (Goei et al., 2003) found liking mattered more than obligation, so the mechanism is debated. The episode says only what was observed.
- **Narrated:** "Sociologist Alvin Gouldner described a near-universal norm: when someone helps us, we feel we owe them." / "In one classic 1971 study, people given a free soft drink by a stranger later bought about twice as many of his raffle tickets as people given nothing."

## 2. Cognitive dissonance (Chapter 4, "Force 2"; the sticky notes)
- **Festinger, L. (1957).** *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance.* Stanford University Press.
- **Festinger, L., & Carlsmith, J. M. (1959).** Cognitive consequences of forced compliance. *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 58*(2), 203–210. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0041593 · PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13640824/
  - Students paid $1 to call a dull task enjoyable later rated it more enjoyable than students paid $20.
- **Vaidis, D. C., et al. (2024).** A multilab replication of the induced-compliance paradigm of cognitive dissonance. *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 7*(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459231213375
  - 39 labs, N = 4,898. It found **no** difference between high-choice and low-choice conditions, a core textbook prediction.
- **Why the episode adds a caveat:** after explaining the idea, the narrator says recent large replications found weaker effects, "so treat it as a tendency, not a law". The on-screen pill cites Vaidis et al. (2024).

## 3. Pluralistic ignorance and organisational silence (Chapter 3 stand-up; Chapter 4, "Force 3")
- **Katz, D., & Allport, F. H. (1931).** *Students' Attitudes.* The term is usually credited to Allport and Katz, around 1931. Text: https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Allport/1931/TOC.htm (publisher detail unverified)
- **Prentice, D. A., & Miller, D. T. (1993).** Pluralistic ignorance and alcohol use on campus. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 64*(2), 243–256. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.64.2.243
  - Princeton students believed they were more uncomfortable with campus drinking than the average student was.
- **Morrison, E. W., & Milliken, F. J. (2000).** Organizational silence. *Academy of Management Review, 25*(4), 706–725. https://doi.org/10.2307/259200
  - A theory paper: employees collectively withhold concerns when they believe speaking up is futile or risky. (Whether it uses the term "pluralistic ignorance" explicitly is unverified.)

## 4. Foot in the door and the slippery slope (Chapter 4 bridge)
- **Freedman, J. L., & Fraser, S. C. (1966).** Compliance without pressure: The foot-in-the-door technique. *JPSP, 4*(2), 195–202. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0023552
  - About 17% of homeowners agreed to a large "Drive Carefully" lawn sign when asked cold, versus about 76% after first agreeing to a small sign.
- **Burger, J. M. (1999).** The foot-in-the-door compliance procedure. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 3*(4), 303–325. The effect is real but conditional and modest in meta-analysis.
- **Welsh, D. T., Ordóñez, L. D., Snyder, D. G., & Christian, M. S. (2015).** The slippery slope. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 100*(1), 114–127. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0036950
  - Small unethical acts that escalate gradually led to larger ones more often than abrupt opportunities did.

## 5. Background (shaped the resolution; not narrated)
- **Edmondson, A. (1999).** Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. *Administrative Science Quarterly, 44*(2), 350–383. https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999. Teams that feel safe raising problems do more of the learning behaviour that catches errors. This informed Ruth's "Next time, Tuesday" and the logging rule.
- **Jecker, J., & Landy, D. (1969).** Liking a person as a function of doing him a favor. *Human Relations, 22*(4), 371–378 (the "Ben Franklin effect"). It was considered and left out: it is a single small study with thin replication.

## What the episode deliberately does not do
- It does not diagnose Graham or call him a narcissist, psychopath or similar. His intent stays open ("Daniel never found out for certain").
- It does not say favours are manipulation. It says the opposite: "Most favours are just kindness. The warning sign is a favour that asks you to hide something."
- It invents no statistics. Every number on screen is either a cited study result or a labelled fictional company figure. The fictional figures are internally consistent and checked by `tests/test_quiet_game.py`.
