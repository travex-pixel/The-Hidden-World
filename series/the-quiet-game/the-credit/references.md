# References and research notes: "The Credit"

The story is fiction; the psychology is real. Citations were checked on 2026-10-11 against bibliographic records, abstracts and reputable secondary sources. Full papers could not be opened from the research environment. Anything unconfirmed is marked **unverified**. All effects are **tendencies, not rules**, and most come from lab studies with student samples.

## 1. Cryptomnesia (Chapter 4, "Force 1")
- **Brown, A. S., & Murphy, D. R. (1989).** Cryptomnesia: Delineating inadvertent plagiarism. *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 15*(3), 432–442. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.15.3.432
  - Groups of four took turns naming category examples. Later, people sometimes listed others' items as their own, or offered them as "new". Item-level rates were modest: later work cites about 9.8% in Experiment 3. Popular claims of "75% of participants" are **unverified** and not used.
- **Marsh, R. L., & Bower, G. H. (1993).** Eliciting cryptomnesia: Unconscious plagiarism in a puzzle task. *JEP: LMC, 19*(3), 673–688. Participants later claimed words found by a computer partner.
- **Stark, L.-J., Perfect, T. J., & Newstead, S. E. (2005).** When elaboration leads to appropriation. *Memory, 13*(6), 561–573. https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/when-elaboration-leads-to-appropriation-unconscious-plagiarism-in/
  - Generating *improvements* to a partner's idea increased later claiming it. Hearing it again or imagining it did not.
- **Perfect, T. J., & Stark, L.-J. (2012).** *Europe's Journal of Psychology, 8*(2). https://ejop.psychopen.eu/index.php/ejop/article/view/459. Improving ideas roughly doubled later plagiarism.
- **Narrated:** "In a 1989 study, people took turns naming ideas in small groups. Later, some of them listed their partners' ideas as their own." / "Later studies found that improving someone else's idea makes you more likely to claim it later."

## 2. Source monitoring
- **Johnson, M. K., Hashtroudi, S., & Lindsay, D. S. (1993).** Source monitoring. *Psychological Bulletin, 114*(1), 3–28. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.114.1.3
  - Memories aren't stored with a source label. We infer the source when we remember, so source errors are normal.
- **Narrated:** "Our memories don't come with a label saying who said what. The brain rebuilds the source each time we remember, and it can get it wrong."

## 3. Egocentric bias (Chapter 4, "Force 2")
- **Ross, M., & Sicoly, F. (1979).** Egocentric biases in availability and attribution. *JPSP, 37*(3), 322–336. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.3.322
  - 37 married couples rated their share of responsibility for 20 activities on a line scale, not in percentages. **27 of 37 couples** overestimated.
  - The episode's 58% and 56% bars are therefore labelled **"Illustration"** on screen, and the narration says the claims "came to more than the whole".
- **Caruso, E. M., Epley, N., & Bazerman, M. H. (2006).** *JPSP, 91*(5), 857–871. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.5.857
  - Authors of multi-author papers believed they contributed more of the total work than is logically possible. Considering others' contributions reduced this.
- **Savitsky, Van Boven, Epley & Wight (2005).** *JESP, 41*(5), 447–457. Overclaiming shrank when people thought about each collaborator individually (school and student samples).

## 4. Unequal credit for ideas
- **McClean, E. J., Martin, S. R., Emich, K. J., & Woodruff, T. (2018).** The social consequences of voice. *Academy of Management Journal, 61*(5), 1869–1891. https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2016.0148
  - Men who offered constructive, idea-based suggestions gained status and leader emergence. Women offering the same kind of suggestions did not. This summary is from the abstract; effect sizes are unverified.
- **Narrated:** "In one study, men who offered new ideas were seen as more leader-like. Women offering the same kind of ideas didn't get that boost."

## 5. Amplification (Chapter 7)
- **Eilperin, J. (2016, 13 September).** *The Washington Post.* Republished 18 Sept 2016 as "How female White House staffers cracked the code of power": https://www.dailyherald.com/20160918/business/how-female-white-house-staffers-cracked-the-code-of-power/
  - Women staffers repeated each other's key points and credited the author, so the points couldn't be ignored or claimed by others.
  - The exact original headline and washingtonpost.com URL are **unverified**. This is journalism based on staff accounts, not a controlled study.

## What the episode deliberately does not do
- It does not call Daniel a liar. His lapse is presented as a memory error, and he corrects it himself.
- It does not prove Graham's intent ("Maybe he hadn't heard Daniel. Maybe he had.").
- It invents no statistics. Company figures are fictional and labelled, consistent with Episode 1, and checked by `tests/test_quiet_game.py`.
