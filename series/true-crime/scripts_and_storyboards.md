# Scripts and storyboards — THE HIDDEN WORLD: Case Files

Both videos share one design system: night-desk backgrounds, Courier Prime case-file typography, Fraunces headlines, Nunito word-by-word captions at y = 1420 (above the Shorts title overlay) with key words in amber, red reserved for evidence, ILLUSTRATION tags on every reconstruction, one documentary narrator (Piper LibriTTS-R speaker 666) and -14 LUFS loudness. Case 01 is graded warm (desert and amber); Case 02 is graded cold (blue newsprint).

## Video 01 — The Jacket That Freed Lindy Chamberlain  (`true_crime_01.mp4`)

**YouTube title:** She Got Life for Murder. The 'Blood' Was Sound-Deadener. #Shorts  
**Runtime:** 81.4 s  
**Central question:** How did a mother convicted of murdering her baby come to be cleared, and what was the 'blood' in her car?

### Opening hooks considered

1. "A mother was jailed for life for murdering her baby. But the 'baby's blood' in her car was never blood."  
   *SELECTED. It opens on the most consequential verified paradox (a life sentence resting on evidence that was not what it seemed) and poses a question the video answers: what was it, and how did the truth come out?*
2. "A missing baby's jacket, found by accident five years later, set a convicted mother free."  
   *Strong, but it spends the turning point in the first second and loses the forensic payoff.*
3. "She said a dingo took her baby. Australia didn't believe her, until a stranger's fall from Uluru."  
   *Emotive, but 'Australia didn't believe her' generalises public opinion we cannot measure, and it leans on a death as a teaser.*

### Case timeline (verified)
- 17 Aug 1980: nine-week-old Azaria disappears from the family tent at the Uluru campsite; Lindy says a dingo took her.
- Late Aug 1980: Azaria's jumpsuit is found near the base of the rock; the matinee jacket is not.
- Feb 1981: first inquest finds a dingo took her. The finding is quashed, and a second inquest commits Lindy for trial.
- 29 Oct 1982: Lindy is convicted of murder (life with hard labour); Michael is convicted as an accessory.
- 22 Feb 1984: the High Court dismisses the appeal 3-2.
- Early 1986: a tourist falls to his death from Uluru. On 2 Feb, searchers find the matinee jacket near a dingo lair.
- 7 Feb 1986: Lindy is released (sentence remitted).
- 1986-87: Morling Royal Commission. The 'blood spray' was factory sound-deadener; no blood is found in the car.
- 15 Sep 1988: all convictions are quashed.
- 1995: third inquest, open finding.
- 12 Jun 2012: fourth inquest. Cause of death: 'attacked and taken by a dingo'.

### Full narration

> A mother was jailed for life for murdering her baby.
> But the 'baby's blood' in her car was never blood.
> August 1980. A campsite at Uluru.
> Lindy Chamberlain said a dingo took her nine week old daughter, Azaria, from their tent.
> Azaria was never found. Her jumpsuit was, near the rock.
> But not the matinee jacket Lindy said Azaria wore over it.
> Prosecutors said she killed Azaria in the family car,
> and that tests found a baby's blood. Even a spray of it, under the dashboard.
> October 1982: guilty of murder. Life, with hard labour.
> In 1984, the High Court rejected her appeal, three votes to two.
> Then, in early 1986, a tourist fell to his death from Uluru.
> Searching near dingo dens below the rock, police found something else.
> Azaria's matinee jacket.
> Five days later, Lindy was released from prison.
> Then a Royal Commission re-examined the science.
> The 'blood spray' under the dashboard? Sound-deadening compound, applied at the factory.
> No blood was found in the car at all.
> In 1988, all the convictions were quashed.
> But only in 2012 did a coroner finally rule how Azaria died:
> attacked and taken by a dingo.
> Just as her mother said, almost thirty-two years earlier.

## Video 02 — The Unabomber Was Caught by His Own Words  (`true_crime_02.mp4`)

**YouTube title:** How the Unabomber's Own Words Gave Him Away #Shorts  
**Runtime:** 84.1 s  
**Central question:** After 17 years with no suspect, how did the FBI finally identify the Unabomber?

### Opening hooks considered

1. "For seventeen years, the FBI hunted a bomber it couldn't name. What finally caught him? His own words. And his own brother."  
   *SELECTED. A clear premise (17 years, no name) and a two-part answer that is itself a curiosity gap: how could words, and a brother, do it?*
2. "In 1995, a serial bomber made an offer: publish my 35,000-word manifesto and I'll stop. The FBI said yes."  
   *Strong, but 'the FBI said yes' oversimplifies (the AG and FBI recommended; newspapers decided), and it hides the family story.*
3. "One old saying, written backwards, helped catch the Unabomber."  
   *Intriguing, but it overstates the idiom's role: it was one strand of the analysis, not the sole basis for the warrant.*

### Case timeline (verified)
- May 1978: first device, linked to Northwestern University (Chicago area).
- 1978-1995: 16 bombs; 3 killed (Hugh Scrutton 1985, Thomas Mosser 1994, Gilbert Murray 1995); 23-24 injured.
- Apr-Jun 1995: the bomber offers to 'desist from terrorism' if his manuscript is published.
- 19 Sep 1995: The Washington Post prints the 35,000-word manifesto (cost shared with the NYT), on the recommendation of AG Reno and FBI Director Freeh.
- Late 1995: Linda Patrik suspects her brother-in-law. David Kaczynski compares the text with Ted's letters; an outside analyst judges them probably the same author.
- Early 1996: the family's lawyer contacts the FBI. FBI analysts compare the writings, including the inverted idiom.
- 3 Apr 1996: arrest at the cabin near Lincoln, Montana; a live bomb is found inside.
- 22 Jan 1998: guilty plea. 4 May 1998: four life terms plus 30 years.
- Aug 1998: David receives the $1M reward and pledges most of it to the victims' families.
- 10 Jun 2023: Kaczynski dies in federal custody.

### Full narration

> For seventeen years, the FBI hunted a bomber it couldn't name.
> What finally caught him? His own words. And his own brother.
> Between 1978 and 1995, sixteen bombs killed three people and injured more than twenty.
> In 1995, he made an offer.
> Publish his 35,000-word manifesto, and he would "desist from terrorism."
> The FBI and the Attorney General backed printing it,
> hoping someone would recognise the writer.
> On September 19th, 1995, The Washington Post printed all of it.
> One reader, Linda Patrik, suspected her husband's brother.
> She urged her husband, David, to read it.
> David compared it with old letters from his brother, Ted.
> An outside analyst judged the same person had probably written both.
> Early in 1996, through a lawyer, the family went to the FBI.
> Analysts found the same unusual twist on an old saying in both:
> "You can't eat your cake and have it too."
> April 3rd, 1996. Agents arrested Ted Kaczynski at his tiny cabin in Montana.
> Inside, they found a live bomb, wrapped and ready to mail.
> In 1998, he pleaded guilty, and was sentenced to life without release.
> David received a one-million-dollar reward.
> He pledged most of it to the families of his brother's victims.
> The bomber wanted the world to read his words. His own family did.

*214 words, spoken in 84.1 s including pauses and holds.*
