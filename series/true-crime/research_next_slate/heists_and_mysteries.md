## 1. The Great Train Robbery (UK, 8 August 1963)

### Hook
The gang that pulled off Britain's biggest train robbery hid on a farm and passed the time playing Monopoly. They left their fingerprints on the board and a ketchup bottle.

### Verified turning point
- **The hideout was found 13 Aug 1963, five days after the robbery.**
  - PC John Woolley found it after a tip-off. Oxford Times names the tipster as herdsman John Maris(s) [GTR3]. ITN/Channel 4's archive coverage runs "from the 8th August through to the 13th, when the Leatherslade Farm hideout was discovered" [GTR4]. ITV 2013 on officers commended [GTR5].
  - The gang left sooner than planned and abandoned evidence [GTR3, GTR6].
- **Fingerprints on everyday objects. Two-plus reputable sources:**
  - BBC obituary of Ronnie Biggs: his "fingerprints were also found on a ketchup bottle at their hideout, Leatherslade Farm" [GTR7].
  - Time: police "found everyone's fingerprints, including some on the Monopoly board" [GTR8].
  - NBC News: the gang was convicted after "their fingerprints were found on a board game they had played while hiding" [GTR9].
  - Crime Library (secondary): Biggs's prints were on a ketchup bottle, beer bottles and Monopoly pieces [GTR10].
  - Mental Floss (secondary): prints on the board and on a ketchup bottle [GTR11].
- **The physical Monopoly board still exists.**
  - It is on permanent display at the Thames Valley Police Museum (Sulhamstead), with **fingerprint powder residue still visible**, per local parish council and village notices [GTR12, GTR13].
  - The exhibits were rediscovered in 1983 in boxes labelled "Great Train Robbery Exhibits" at a Met Police Museum warehouse [GTR12].
  - These are community notices, not the museum's own page, so treat it as **likely, single-type sourcing**. Confirm with Thames Valley Police before stating it on screen.
- **Biggs's escape.** On **8 July 1965** he went over the Wandsworth Prison wall on a rope ladder into a waiting furniture van after serving about 15 months of a 30-year term [GTR14, GTR15].
  - Police said it was "engineered without a doubt with collusion inside the prison" [GTR15].

### Legal status (today)
- Closed. The convictions date from 1964. Biggs's sentence ended with compassionate release (2009) and his death (2013) [GTR19, GTR20].
- Bill Boal and Lennie Field were later exonerated (Wikipedia pointer) [GTR16].
- **Do not list them as robbers.**

### People and sensitivities
- **Jack Mills, the train driver,** was struck on the head with an iron bar. He never returned to work and died in 1970 [GTR16].
  - Cause of death is **disputed.** One report says leukaemia at 64. His family links it to the attack [GTR16].
  - **This is the main sensitivity.** The robbery was not "victimless", so do not romanticise it.
- **Ronnie Biggs:** fame far exceeds his role (see myths).
- **Bruce Reynolds** ran the operation [GTR22].
- **Exonerated men:** Boal and Field. **Do not depict them as guilty.**

### Myths and exaggerations to avoid
- **MYTH: "Biggs was the mastermind."** Crime Library says that "contrary to popular belief, Biggs did not take part in the actual planning… and in fact played a fairly minor role" [GTR10, GTR22].
  - His job was reportedly to bring a replacement train driver ("Old Pete"), who could not drive the locomotive (Wikipedia mirror; treat as secondary) [GTR22].
- **UNVERIFIED: "They played Monopoly with the real stolen money."**
  - Repeated widely: Oxford Times ("playing with real £5 notes") and the police-museum notice ("reputed to have used real money") [GTR3, GTR12].
  - It is folklore-adjacent. Use "reportedly" or "legend has it".
- **Oversimplification: "One Monopoly fingerprint convicted the gang."** Prints were found on several items (ketchup bottle, beer bottles, board, pieces) [GTR7, GTR10]. Use "fingerprints on the Monopoly set and a ketchup bottle".
- **"Nobody was hurt."** False. Mills was assaulted [GTR16].
- **UNVERIFIED: "Mills died of his injuries."** Disputed; see above.
- **Minority theory (Glasgow's Arthur Thompson as mastermind, 2020s book):** not established [GTR22].

### Audience signals
- **BBC Sounds / Radio 5 Live, *Gangster Presents: The Story of Ronnie Biggs*** (6 parts, narrated by Daniel Mays). Debuted **7 May 2026**, with previously unheard recordings made by Biggs in exile [GTR23, GTR24].
- **Dan Snow's History Hit podcast**, "The Great Train Robbery" (Aug 2025), with author Nick Russell-Pavier and Colin Mackenzie, the journalist who found Biggs in Brazil [GTR25].
- **Auction news, Aug 2026:** Ewbank's auction of a mailbag signed by 10 of the robbers, a **£500 Monopoly banknote** described as having "helped investigators", and signed 1974 press photos of Biggs and Reynolds [GTR26, GTR27].
- **Anniversaries Oct 2026–Mar 2027:** none round.
  - Biggs's death: 13th anniversary on 18 Dec 2026.
  - Trial opening: 63rd anniversary in Jan 2027.
  - Weak timing.

### Visual potential
- Night-time mail train halted under a bridge, with a red signal and a gloved hand over a lamp.
- Sacks of mailbags.
- A run-down farmhouse.
- **A Monopoly board dusted with fingerprint powder** (a hero prop).
- A ketchup bottle under a magnifier.
- A 1960s detective with a fingerprint card.
- A rope ladder over a prison wall into a furniture van.
- Rio beach postcard.
- A 2001 jet landing.

### Risk rating: MEDIUM
- There is real violence against a named victim (Mills).
- There is a strong pull to glamorise Biggs.
- Legal risk is low: the case is 60+ years old, with convictions and principals deceased. Only Biggs, Mills and Reynolds were checked in this session.

### Verdict: RECOMMEND
- The single-clue shape is perfect: they cleaned the farm badly, and the board game betrayed them.
- Pair it with the Biggs escape as a tag ending.
- Credit Mills, and frame the money-Monopoly detail as legend.

## 2. The Yorkshire Ripper hoax: "Wearside Jack" (1978–79; solved 2005–06)

> **Naming note:** victims' families objected to ITV's original title for *The Long Shadow* because the "Yorkshire Ripper" moniker created a "dark brand" around the killer. ITV renamed the show [WJ12].
> - Recommended episode title: **"The Hoax Tape"** or **"Wearside Jack"**.
> - Refer to the killer as **Peter Sutcliffe** and use the "Ripper" nickname only where necessary for context.

### Hook
A taunting tape, spoken in a Sunderland accent, sent detectives hunting the wrong man 100 miles from the real killer. Twenty-six years later, the saliva on an envelope gave the hoaxer away.

### Verified turning point
- In **2005** a cold-case review tested saliva from the seal or flap of one of the hoax envelopes. The result matched the DNA of **John Samuel Humble**, an unemployed Sunderland man. His sample was on file from an earlier arrest for being drunk and disorderly [WJ1, WJ2, WJ3, WJ4].
- **He pleaded guilty** to four counts of perverting the course of justice, covering three letters and the tape [WJ3, WJ5].
- **The Court of Appeal judgment** is a primary legal record, seen via extract only: *R v Humble* [2006] EWCA Crim 2775, handed down 24 Oct 2006 [WJ6].
  - It confirms the four counts and the concurrent sentences: **six years on counts 1–3 and eight years on count 4.**
  - It records that his acts "diverted resources to a search in the North East and allowed the real offender to continue his brutal attacks on women".
  - A case summary says eight years "was not excessive" [WJ7]. That implies the appeal was dismissed, but **the operative order was not seen**.

### What the hoax did (verified to the two-source level unless marked)
- **Hoax material:** three letters and one cassette tape sent to the head of the investigation, Asst Chief Constable **George Oldfield**. A copy was also posted to the Daily Mirror's Manchester office [WJ1, WJ8].
- **The dialect clue:** experts placed the voice in the Castletown area of Sunderland, which is why he was dubbed "Wearside Jack" [WJ1, WJ9].
- **The diversion:**
  - Police focused on the North East. About **40,000 men** were reportedly investigated there [WJ8, WJ10].
  - Peter Sutcliffe was **interviewed nine times** over five years and repeatedly eliminated because he did not have a North-East accent [WJ9].
  - DC **Andrew Laptew**'s report flagging Sutcliffe was downgraded, partly because his handwriting did not match the hoax letters [WJ9].
- **Warnings ignored:**
  - A Northumbria Police DI reported in **Sept 1979** that the tape was likely a hoax. The report was ignored (Wikipedia pointer) [WJ9].
  - **Single-source/UNVERIFIED:** the FBI and profiler Robert Ressler told West Yorkshire Police the tape was a hoax [WJ8].
- **The timing problem:** the third letter (March 1979) arrived "only 13 days before" Sutcliffe killed **Josephine Whitaker** [WJ8, WJ10].
- **Byford Report:** this government review of the investigation was fully published in 2006 and confirmed the criticisms [WJ9].
- **Sentencing judge, Norman Jones QC:** Humble's behaviour "may well have contributed" to the killer remaining at large [WJ3, WJ6].

### Legal status (today)
- **Closed.** Humble was convicted in 2006, his sentence appeal was heard in Oct 2006, and he died in 2019 [WJ6, WJ13].
- Peter Sutcliffe was convicted in 1981 of 13 murders and died in prison. Both facts are background knowledge, **not verified this session**.

### People and sensitivities (HIGH)
- **The 13 murdered women, the survivors and their families.**
  - The story should be about the hoax and the police failure, **not the attacks.** Do not depict violence or crime scenes, or use victims' images without a clear licence.
  - If victims are named, name them with dignity and briefly. Josephine Whitaker is the one name tied to the hoax timeline in the sources.
- **The "three lives" claim.** A regional headline says the hoax "cost £1 million and three innocent lives" [WJ10]. Others say it "allowed Sutcliffe to murder three other victims" [WJ2].
  - That is a **counterfactual**: no court found that specific women would have survived.
  - Script-safe: "Sutcliffe killed three more women before he was caught."
  - The names Barbara Leach, Marguerite Walls and Jacqueline Hill are background knowledge and **were not verified this session**.
- **Survivors' accounts.** Some survivors told police the attacker had a local Yorkshire accent and were not heeded [WJ9 pointer]. This is a strong but sensitive beat.
- **Humble.** He is deceased (2019) and had chronic alcohol problems [WJ13]. Avoid mockery.
- **Nickname.** See the naming note at the top of this case.

### Myths and exaggerations to avoid
- **Accent.** He is often called "Geordie" (Newcastle). The sources say **Wearside/Sunderland** (Castletown) [WJ1, WJ9]. Use "Sunderland accent".
- **DNA arrest year.** It varies (1991, 2000 or 2001); see the key-dates conflict note.
- **Charge.** It was **perverting the course of justice** (four counts), not "wasting police time" [WJ5, WJ6].
- **"The hoax was the only reason Sutcliffe wasn't caught."** False. Multiple failures are documented in the Byford Report, including record-keeping and the ignored Laptew report [WJ9].
- **Ressler/FBI warning:** single source. Mark UNVERIFIED.

### Audience signals
- **ITV, *The Long Shadow* (2023):** a seven-part drama written in consultation with the victims' families, including the hoax plot [WJ12, WJ15]. It is older than the 2024–26 window but drove recent interest.
- **No 2024–2026 documentary or podcast confirmed** in searches [WJ16]. A YouTube explainer possibly from 2024 was not confirmed.
- Older programmes: BBC Four's *The Yorkshire Ripper Files* (2019, Liza Williams), with an episode on Humble [WJ17], and podcast episodes from 2021 and later [WJ16].
- **Anniversaries Oct 2026–Mar 2027:** none round.
  - Humble's arrest: 21st anniversary in Oct 2026.
  - Humble's sentencing: the 20th anniversary passed in March 2026.
  - Weak timing.

### Visual potential
- A 1970s cassette recorder and a spinning tape.
- Police press-conference microphones.
- A map with an arrow sweeping from West Yorkshire to Wearside.
- An envelope flap and a cotton swab.
- A DNA helix resolving over a 2005 database screen.
- A 1979 incident room with card index boxes; the paper mountain is a strong motif.
- Leeds Crown Court exterior.
- No victim depictions.

### Risk rating: MEDIUM
- Sensitivity is **high.** The case involves a serial murderer of women, families object to the brand, and the counterfactual claims are risky.
- Legal risk is **low.** The principals are dead and the convictions are final.
- It is manageable with strict framing.

### Verdict: RECOMMEND, WITH CONDITIONS
- This is the closest structural match to BTK's floppy disk: a forensic trace on a taunting letter, decades later.
- Conditions:
  - No "Ripper" in the title.
  - No depiction of attacks.
  - Use the "three more women" wording.
  - Run a pre-publication sensitivity check.

## 3. The Hatton Garden heist (London, Easter 2015)

### Hook
A gang of pensioners drilled into a London vault over Easter. Their own car and their own boasting in the pub gave them away.

### Verified turning point
- **The car.**
  - The Flying Squad trawled CCTV (BuzzFeed says about 150 cameras) and spotted a distinctive **white Mercedes E-class with a black roof and black alloys** parked near the vault [HG1].
  - Running its number plate through **London's congestion-charge database** led to **John "Kenny" Collins** [HG1].
  - Crime & Investigation independently confirms that police "pinpointed one of their vehicles using CCTV, then planted listening devices" [HG2].
